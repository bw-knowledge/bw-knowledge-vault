In summary, the hospitality industry's chronic delay in adopting emerging media platforms—from Instagram to TikTok to Substack—has become financially indefensible as the travel booking funnel flattens into social platforms, search consolidates around brand recognition, and the window for organic audience growth closes.

## Headlines

- **Hospitality brands must establish social media presence now or lose organic growth forever.** The hospitality industry's pattern of arriving late to media platforms—from Instagram in 2010 to TikTok today—has shifted from a competitive disadvantage to a structural threat as booking behavior migrates directly into social platforms through features like TikTok GO.

- **Google's AI Overviews are dismantling the paid search funnel hospitality brands depend on.** Zero-click searches now account for 58% of Google queries, click-through rates have dropped significantly, and generic search traffic is collapsing, leaving only branded queries viable—forcing brands to build recognition through owned and social channels.

- **Luxury hospitality can no longer claim demographic or format objections to platforms like TikTok.** Users over 30 now represent 38% of TikTok's base, up from 22% in 2021, and luxury competitors in fashion and goods have demonstrated that sophisticated brands succeed by maintaining their identity rather than mimicking platform conventions.

- **Owned channels like Substack offer hospitality brands irreplaceable guest relationships independent of algorithmic change.** Email subscribers grant explicit permission and signal intent, creating direct relationships that OTAs and social platforms cannot replicate and that survive algorithm deprecation or platform shifts.

- **Hospitality's window to build organic presence before transactional booking layers mature is closing rapidly.** Follower growth on brand accounts slowed 33% in 2025, and early movers have compounded advantage—brands delaying entry will face higher costs and lower returns on social channel investment.

# Hospitality Is Always Late to the Media Party

### This is no longer a defensible or an affordable position

[![The House Standard's avatar](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NFAX!,w_36,h_36,c_fill,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F28f06077-fc69-4882-96a5-a9f28ea34048_2500x2500.png)](https://substack.com/@housestandard)

As someone who genuinely loves both media and hospitality, and built a career at the intersection of the two, I harbor a major grudge: our industry, while innovative on so many levels, is truly archaic in its approach to media. Always the follower, never the leader. Let me use a simple example from earlier in my career to illustrate.

Instagram launched in 2010 as a new and exciting platform, a natural fit for brands in highly visual categories. Fashion moved first. Food and beverage moved next. Retail followed. By 2011, the likes of Nike, Burberry and Starbucks were treating it as a serious channel. By late 2013, Michael Kors had become the first brand to run a sponsored post on the platform, gaining 34,000 followers in 18 hours. Consumer goods and fashion had staked their claim on Instagram before most hospitality brands had held their first internal meeting about it.

There was one notable exception, and I’ll admit it’s personal. Starwood, where I spent the early years of my career, was the first hotel company to advertise on Instagram in January 2014, the first to integrate guest photos across all property websites, and the first major hotel brand to publish unedited guest reviews. One company pulling an entire industry forward. The rest of hospitality spent the next two years reading the Starwood case studies and debating whether to follow.

That was more than fifteen years ago. The platforms have changed, but the pattern hasn’t.

Luxury hospitality sells one of the most aspirational products in the world: the experience of being somewhere exceptional, looked after, transported from your ordinary life. And yet it treats the story of that product like an afterthought. The gap between what this industry spends on the product and what it invests in communicating why that product matters has been growing for years. TikTok GO is just the latest proof that the gap is becoming a liability.

#### **The Traditional Marketing Funnel Is No Longer Relevant**

Last week, TikTok launched TikTok GO in the US. Users can now discover and book hotels, attractions, and tours directly inside the app, through partnerships with Booking.com, Expedia Group, Viator, GetYourGuide, and others. The platform is positioning it as the shortest path from inspiration to booking: no browser, no new tab, no friction.

Before we go further, let’s be clear about what this product actually is: another OTA layer. A booking through TikTok GO still carries the standard Booking.com commission plus a creator commission on top. For brands already fighting to own the direct guest relationship, routing bookings through a native social feature is structurally counterproductive. I am _not_ advocating for its immediate adoption.

The more important question isn’t whether to use the feature. It’s what the feature signals about where the travel funnel is heading. Travel discovery has been happening on social platforms for years. What’s changing is that booking behavior is following. The funnel is no longer awareness to Google search to direct booking. It is flattening, with discovery, consideration, and transaction increasingly living in the same place. TikTok GO is the formalization of a shift already underway, and it will not be the last platform to make this move. Instagram, we’re watching.

The brands that aren’t present on TikTok won’t just miss the booking feature when it matures. They will miss the opportunity to redirect that booking to a direct channel. No organic foothold, no audience, no relationship to leverage when the moment arrives. You cannot retroactively build presence, and you cannot win while perpetually playing catch-up.

The evidence is already visible on Instagram, a platform that solidified its place as the destination for travel inspiration years ago. Hospitality brands are still there trying to build audiences that individual creators assembled by moving quickly on Reels while the industry was debating whether video was worth the investment. These are the same brands overspending on paid search and wondering where the ROI has gone. The answer is the digital junkyard. You cannot operate in the media landscape of 2026 as if it’s 2016 and expect the same results.

#### **The Avoidance Pattern**

I’ve been in conversations about TikTok with hospitality brands for years, particularly in the boutique and luxury space. The reasons not to engage have been consistent, and for a time, understandable: our customer skews older and wealthier and is therefore not on TikTok, the format isn’t aligned with luxury, we don’t have the resources to produce video.

In 2026, most hospitality brands with interesting stories to tell are still absent from the platform. A quick audit confirms it. The only brands with any meaningful presence are large chains, Hilton, Marriott, Choice Hotels, with a small showing from established luxury names, most notably Fairmont and Ritz-Carlton. Four Seasons has an account with 68,000 followers and not a single post. Several others have started accounts and apparently abandoned them after one or two posts. One exception worth naming is Equinox Hotel New York, which has been gradually and thoughtfully building a presence that genuinely reflects the brand.

Current hospitality presence on TikTok is thin, sporadic, and leaves out most of the players that would benefit most from diversifying their distribution. The objections haven’t changed. The platform and consumer behavior have.

TikTok reached an estimated 1.9 billion monthly active users by early 2026, adding 300 million users in 2025 alone. Average time spent is 61 minutes per day. Engagement rates run at 3.80%, compared to Instagram Reels at 0.65%. Over 7 million US businesses now use TikTok for marketing. The platform’s ad revenue grew from $2.6 billion in 2020 to $23 billion in 2024. That last figure is not a reflection of user behavior but brands arriving en masse. The pattern is not new. First come the users, then the creators, then the brands.

Hospitality has watched this sequence play out on every major platform for two decades and still arrives late every time. The difference now is that the window for organic growth is closing. Follower growth rates for brand accounts slowed 33% on average in 2025. The brands that showed up early compounded. The ones still waiting for proof are missing the momentum.

#### **The Demographic Argument Is Over**

Luxury’s last defensible objection to TikTok, and to emerging media generally, has always been the audience. It’s young, it’s unsophisticated. These are not the people we want staying at our properties. That argument is now empirically finished.

Users 30 and older make up 38% of TikTok’s user base, up from 22% in 2021. The platform’s fastest-growing segment is 25 to 34 year olds. Going from 22% to 38% over-30 users in four years is not a gradual drift. It is the audience the industry has been dismissing arriving in force.

But the more important argument isn’t who is on TikTok today. It’s who is forming brand preferences right now that will determine booking behavior a decade from now.

Luxury has always understood that you build a brand long before you build a customer. The 32-year-old watching Four Seasons content today is the guest staying there at 45. If you are not present during those years of aspiration formation, you are not in the consideration set when the purchase happens. And by the time that absence shows up in occupancy numbers, it is not a media problem anymore. It is a brand problem.

The luxury brands that have figured this out are mostly not in hospitality. Loewe is widely considered the most strategically sophisticated luxury brand on TikTok. Their content doesn’t read as brand content. It slots into the feed like a creator you already follow, with organic participation and cinematic visuals that belong entirely to the brand’s identity. They are not using the platform as a sales channel. They are using it to own the cultural conversation around what Loewe means. Louis Vuitton has 13.1 million followers. Burberry leads with a community-first, UGC amplification strategy that is [genuinely delightful.](https://www.tiktok.com/@burberry/video/7641163479864429846)

None of them waited for the platform to prove itself. They understood that being aspirational requires being original, and weren’t afraid to use a new platform as a testing ground, which, by the way, is considerably easier when the audience is small and expectations are low.

#### **Search Is No Longer the Cash Register It Once Was**

While hospitality has been slow to build brand presence on social, the channel most brands defaulted to, paid search, is undergoing a structural shift that isn’t reversible.

At Google I/O this week, Google confirmed what the data has been signaling for two years. AI Overviews are now built directly into search results. Agents scan the web on the user’s behalf. Custom interfaces answer queries without requiring a click. Google’s stated direction is a search experience where users may never need to visit your website.

The numbers preceding this announcement were already uncomfortable. 58% of searches end with zero clicks. Click-through rates on pages triggering AI Overviews have dropped significantly. Google referral traffic to media sites fell 33% last year, with further declines projected.

The brands holding ground, and in some cases gaining, are the ones people already know by name. Branded queries with AI Overviews see a lift in click-through rate. Generic queries are declining. Named brands are consolidating value (State of Brand provides an excellent [analysis](https://www.thestateofbrand.com/news/google-just-killed-search-as-we-know-it)).

If your media strategy is built primarily on buying clicks for generic travel intent, the floor is dropping. If you haven’t invested in being a brand people actively search for by name, there is no fallback position when the paid channel thins out. Social presence and owned channels are not alternatives to performance marketing. They are what make performance marketing defensible and cost-effective over time.

#### **Owning the Conversation**

Last week, **[Ace Hotel](https://open.substack.com/users/314119974-ace-hotel?utm_source=mentions)** joined Substack, becoming the first hospitality brand to establish a presence on a platform with over 20 million monthly active subscribers and nearly 50 million unique monthly visitors. Seriously, the first hotel brand. It’s a meaningful move, because it represents a fundamentally different understanding of what media is for. Media is not a fight for a few seconds of attention with a transactional outcome as the goal. It is an ongoing conversation and a genuine relationship with your guest. Build the community, build the connection, and the transactions follow when the guest is ready to book.

A guest who subscribes to your newsletter has given you something no media buy can replicate: permission, an inbox, and a clear signal of intent. That relationship belongs to you. It does not disappear when a platform changes its algorithm or deprecates organic reach.

Owned channels also give you something paid media simply cannot: real estate. Space to actually say something. A TikTok presence built around a genuine point of view lets you articulate who you are, what you believe, and what makes your property worth the price. A Substack publication lets you maintain an ongoing conversation with guests between stays, build anticipation for the next visit, and establish the kind of insider relationship no OTA can replicate.

An independent hotel is one of the most compelling brand stories in any category. The history, the design, the curation, the staff, the food, all of it is an expression of a point of view that someone made intentional. That story has real value, particularly in today’s experience economy. A keyword bid has nowhere to put it.

There is also a pragmatic case for Substack specifically: every hospitality brand is already doing email marketing. The tone of voice, the content strategy, and the dedicated resource are already there. The question is simply whether to use them to reach new audiences rather than recycle the same messaging to the same list.

Ace aside, the space is wide open for a hospitality brand to build a media presence as intricate and compelling as the properties themselves. Rather than merely chasing mentions in legacy publications, the smart brands will begin building their own media ecosystems.

#### **Showing Up on Your Own Terms**

There is a legitimate version of the luxury objection to TikTok, and it deserves a direct answer. The platform’s native content grammar, fast cuts, trending audio, creator-first aesthetics, does work against the restraint and intentionality that defines luxury communication. The brands that have failed on TikTok failed because they tried to speak the platform’s language rather than their own.

The counterargument is that luxury brands are, by definition, masters of differentiation. The most celebrated names in luxury hospitality today, Aman, Belmond, Oetker, built their reputations precisely by deviating from category norms. The same instinct applies to media. True innovation is not showing up on a platform and mimicking everyone else. It is showing up in a way that is unmistakably yours. The only hard rule on TikTok is that it is a video platform. Everything else is the creator’s call.

To be clear, this is not an argument for chasing every new platform or producing content for the sake of presence. It is a case for a deliberate rethinking of where brand value gets built and an honest accounting of what the current media mix is and is not producing. The argument that video content is too expensive or too inaccessible no longer holds in 2026: if a teenager with a phone and a tripod can build an audience of millions, so can your brand. The same is true for every medium where hospitality brands have cited resources as the reason for staying out.

TikTok, Substack, or whatever platform emerges next, none of it is the point. The point is that the booking funnel is moving to social, search is consolidating around brand recognition, and the brands absent from both conversations are growing more dependent on paid channels that are getting more expensive and less effective. The window to build organic presence before the booking layer fully arrives is open now. It will not stay open.

The alternative is the position hospitality has occupied for twenty years: arriving late, paying commission, and wondering why the guest relationship belongs to a third party instead of the property.
