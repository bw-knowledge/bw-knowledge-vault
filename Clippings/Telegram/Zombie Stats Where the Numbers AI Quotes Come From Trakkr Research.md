In summary, AI assistants frequently cite statistics without tracing them back to original research, with only 40% of traced statistics reaching a genuine study, while zombie statistics—numbers repeated across the web with no original source—spread because AI models amplify secondhand sources like blogs and roundups that lack proper attribution.

## Headlines

- **Only 40% of statistics cited by AI assistants trace back to original research.** ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, and Google AI Overviews were asked 200 questions requiring numerical answers; when researchers followed citation trails up to five pages deep, they found that 40% reached a matching study, 8% reached a study with altered numbers or context, 40% hit pages that couldn't be verified, and 11% led nowhere.

- **Zombie statistics propagate because AI cites company blogs and roundups instead of original research.** Of cited sources, 54% were company blogs (37% sourced), 13% were research firms (62% sourced), 10% were news outlets (37% sourced), and 8% were statistics roundups (35% sourced), meaning AI amplifies secondhand repeating of numbers rather than reaching primary sources.

- **Four statistics quoted by all five AI assistants have no underlying study.** The claims that 44% of salespeople give up after one follow-up, 90% of social media users follow at least one brand, 75% of searchers never scroll past the first page, and that acquiring customers costs 5 to 25 times more than retaining them all trail back to pages with no research citation.

- **ChatGPT performed best but still only sourced 57% of its statistics correctly.** While ChatGPT quoted the fewest numbers per answer (1.3 versus 6.8 for Claude), Perplexity followed at 46% sourced, while Google AI Overviews, Gemini, and Claude each sourced 38–39% of their cited statistics.

- **Statistics change as they circulate, with 17% altering digits or context on the way to AI assistants.** A 2004 study of 15 women evaluating health websites transformed into the claim that "94% of first impressions are design-related" for all websites; changes most commonly involved what was measured (20%), the number itself (26%), the year (17%), the source (16%), or who was asked (14%).

1. In the answer
Every assistant got the same question: “What percentage of salespeople give up after one follow-up?” All five assistants gave the same figure, and only ChatGPT said the evidence behind it was unclear.

2. One click in
Google AI Overviews cited a sales firm’s stats page, from 2020. The number is right there. Its “source” link goes to a writing sample.

3. Two clicks in
That leads to a freelance writer’s sample article, online by 2017. The number is in its headline. It lists two slide decks, a blog list and two news stories. None is a study of follow-ups.

4. The end of the trail
That’s where the trail ends. We tried every other route to this number we could find, and each one stopped at a page that cites no research. This is what we mean by a zombie stat: a number that keeps getting repeated with no study behind it.


Most numbers aren’t like this one. The trouble is that you can’t tell which ones are from the answer. This one came with the same confident tone and the same kind of citation as any other, and all five assistants agreed on it. So we followed 1,285 statistics the same way.

We took a random sample of the statistics each assistant cited, 1,285 in all, and walked every trail the same way: the cited page, then that page’s source, up to five pages deep.2**Note 2** Each assistant’s sample was drawn at random from the statistics it cited: ChatGPT 108, Perplexity 277, Gemini 300, Claude 300, Google AI Overviews 300. The five-assistant rate weights each assistant by how many cited statistics it quoted, so it describes the statistics people were shown. Where a page named a source without a link, we searched for it.

40% reached a study that says the same thing. Another 8% reached the study, but the number or its context had changed on the way. The rest never got there: 40% stopped at a source we couldn’t check, because it was locked, gone or silent on the number, and 11% led nowhere at all.3**Note 3** An AI model judged every trail, and a stronger one re-judged 300. Then people re-checked 150 trails at random, with a third check wherever the two disagreed. People found originals the model missed: by the model alone, 23% were sourced. Every rate here is corrected with the hand checks, and its range carries the uncertainty from both.

### If you use them

1. 1


#### Follow the citation, then follow it again.



If the cited page is a list of statistics or a company blog, use its own link to go one page further. Keep going until you reach the study itself, or until you can’t.

2. 2


#### Check who was asked, how many, and when.



Most of the changes we found were in the context, not the digits: one country’s poll, a ten-year-old study, fifteen people.

3. 3


#### Don’t take agreement as a check.



Five assistants giving the same number isn’t five sources. The numbers they repeated most were no better sourced than the rest.


### If you publish them

1. 1


#### Put the method next to the number.



The sample, the date and a link to the source, on the same page as the figure, so the next page, and the next answer, can trace it back to you.

2. 2


#### Keep the findings in the open.



Reports behind a form were a common dead end: even trails that started at a research firm fell short 38% of the time. Put the headline numbers and the method on an open page.

3. 3


#### See which page AI cites for your numbers.



It is often a roundup or a blog that copied you, sometimes with an older figure. That page decides what people hear.

Every question was asked once, on 24 October 2026. Answers change from day to day.

- #### A machine judge


An AI model judged every trail and was stricter than a person. We corrected it with 150 hand checks; on its own it found 23%.

- #### Locked sources


Some originals sit behind paywalls or sign-up forms. We count those as a source we couldn’t check, not as missing.


### Notes

1. A statistic is a share, rate, average, count or cost that an assistant states as fact. We left out advice (“save 20% of your income”) and prices.
2. Each assistant’s sample was drawn at random from the statistics it cited: ChatGPT 108, Perplexity 277, Gemini 300, Claude 300, Google AI Overviews 300. The five-assistant rate weights each assistant by how many cited statistics it quoted, so it describes the statistics people were shown.
3. An AI model judged every trail, and a stronger one re-judged 300. Then people re-checked 150 trails at random, with a third check wherever the two disagreed. People found originals the model missed: by the model alone, 23% were sourced. Every rate here is corrected with the hand checks, and its range carries the uncertainty from both.
4. Google AI Overviews answers to 52,930 everyday English searches that Trakkr tracked between 25 September 2026 and 25 October 2026. 4.4% carried a statistic, and we traced 300 of those the same way. Only counts and rates leave our systems.
5. We name the original studies. The pages that repeated them are described by type, not named. Every step in these trails was checked by hand on the open web, with archived copies kept.
6. We compared each assistant’s headline figure for the same question. A range counts at its middle, and figures within 2 points count as the same.

## Questions

The ones we hear most about this study.

Do AI assistants make up statistics?

Rarely, in our sample. The page an assistant cited contained the figure 82% of the time. The bigger problem is upstream: the pages AI cites are mostly blogs and roundups repeating a number. Of the statistics we traced, four in ten reached a study that says the same thing.

Which AI assistant is most reliable with statistics?

In our sample, ChatGPT did best: 57% of the statistics it quoted reached a matching original, and it quoted the fewest. The other four were between 38% and 46%. Every assistant quoted numbers that led nowhere.

How can I check a statistic an AI gives me?

Open the source it cites. If that page is a list of statistics or a company blog, follow its link to the next page, and keep going until you reach the study itself. Check the year, who was asked and how many, then quote the study, not the page that repeated it.

What is a zombie statistic?

A number that keeps being repeated after its source has changed, aged or never existed. It survives because each page copies the last one, and AI assistants now repeat it to millions of people.
