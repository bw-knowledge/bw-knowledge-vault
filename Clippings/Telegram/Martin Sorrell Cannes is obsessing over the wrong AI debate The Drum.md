In summary, Martin Sorrell argues that the advertising industry is focusing on the wrong AI debate at Cannes Lions—discussing generative AI's impact on creativity when it should be examining how AI is fundamentally restructuring media planning, buying, agency economics, and the need for commercial accountability from marketers.

## Suggested Topics

Agency Strategy, AI in Marketing, Media Buying and Planning

## Matched Topics

[[Generative AI]], [[Strategy]]

## Headlines

- **Agencies must become technology businesses first, not creative ones.** Sorrell contends that the industry's competitive advantage lies in mastering media planning and buying—where holding companies derive their core profits—rather than in generative creative tools that raise the average quality of work across the board.

- **Quantum computing, not generative AI, represents the next major disruption.** While the industry debates AI's creative implications, Sorrell warns that quantum computing poses a seismic shift that almost nobody in marketing is preparing for.

- **CMOs need to prove commercial impact to gain influence with CEOs.** Marketing leaders must become far more accountable for demonstrating direct contribution to business growth rather than simply producing communications.

- **Flat, integrated agency structures outperform siloed organizations.** Sorrell advocates for single-brand models that share information freely rather than competing capability divisions that encourage internal politics and information hoarding.

- **Platforms are becoming more powerful, and agencies must validate their algorithms.** Google, Meta, Amazon, and TikTok are building end-to-end marketing systems; the future agency will help clients navigate these platforms transparently rather than compete with them.

# Martin Sorrell: Cannes is obsessing over the wrong AI debate

![](https://thedrum-media.imgix.net/thedrum-prod/s3/s3fs-public/pictures/picture-150231-1689159175.jpg?w=56&ar=1:1&fit=crop&crop=faces&auto=format)

**The S4 Capital founder argues agencies should focus less on generative creativity and more on how technology is rewriting media, business models and marketing accountability – before quantum becomes the next disruption.**

![](https://thedrum-media.imgix.net/thedrum-user-assets-prod/s3/images/original/img-2677.jpg?w=940&ar=default&fit=crop&crop=faces&auto=format&dpr=2)

If you want to annoy Sir Martin Sorrell, ask him about AI and creativity.

The S4 Capital founder barely lets the question land before explaining why [Cannes Lions](https://www.thedrum.com/topics/cannes-lions) has spent the week debating the wrong problem.

“Cannes is going the wrong way at the moment,” he tells The Drum. “This year it’s about the impact of AI on human creativity and I think that’s going down the wrong rabbit hole.”

Instead, Sorrell believes the biggest disruption facing marketing won’t come from AI-generated advertising but from something far less glamorous: the complete reinvention of media planning, media buying and agency structures. While much of the industry has been debating prompts and pixels on the Croisette, the economics underpinning the holding companies are being rewritten.

Sorrell, who is speaking to The Drum for an upcoming documentary exploring the future of agencies, argues that agencies need to become technology businesses first, creative businesses second. CMOs need to become far more commercially accountable. And while everyone is talking about generative AI, almost nobody is preparing for what he believes is the next seismic shift.

“If we think AI is going to alter the calculus,” he says, “stand by for quantum.”

For Sorrell, that’s the conversation Cannes should already be having. AI may improve the average standard of creative work, he argues, but it doesn’t automatically solve differentiation.

“What AI does do is it raises the general level of creativity, so we’ll get rid of some of the rubbish, but if everybody’s doing personalization at scale, how do I differentiate Coca-Cola’s advertising?

“I think the answer, in the short to medium term, is human creativity.”

The much bigger issue sits elsewhere. “They should be looking at media planning and media buying,” he says. “That’s the gut source of profit for the holding companies.”

Throughout the conversation, Sorrell returns repeatedly to the same point: AI is fundamentally changing how media is bought, measured and optimized, yet too much of the industry remains focused on creative execution.

“The platforms are becoming more powerful. We have to be the friend. There’s very little of the enemy any more.”

Google, Meta, Amazon and TikTok are increasingly building end-to-end marketing systems. The future agency, he believes, won’t compete with them. It will help clients navigate them.

Advertisement

“We have to validate the algorithm. You can’t mark your own homework.”

That means transparency becomes one of the agency’s biggest products. “At the moment, they don’t have transparency. There’s proprietary trading, there’s take rates, there’s all this lack of transparency.”

He is equally blunt about how agencies need to organize themselves. Without naming winners and losers, Sorrell argues that structures built around competing capability silos inevitably encourage internal politics rather than client focus. “You can’t do it by capability. You just build internal division.”

His own blueprint has barely changed. “We started with four basic principles: digital-only; data-driven; faster, better, cheaper; and one Monks.”The last point matters most. “One brand.”

Artificial intelligence, he argues, makes that even more important because knowledge is no longer something that should be hoarded inside organizations.

“AI is democratizing knowledge. You build silos inside an organization by controlling information.”

For him, flatter organizations that share information freely will beat internal empires competing for attribution. The same advice extends to marketers.

“The driver of transformation inside clients is not the CMO. It’s the CFO.”If marketers want greater influence, they have to become much better at proving commercial impact, he says.

“We have to get the CMO into the CEO’s office.”That means validation, measurement and demonstrating that marketing contributes directly to growth rather than simply producing communications.

Sorrell also thinks the industry is underestimating what comes after generative AI. “It’s not just AI, it’s quantum, too. You’ve got blockchain, AI and quantum, but I haven’t even heard the word mentioned.

Advertisement

Despite spending much of the interview dissecting agency structures, technology platforms and geopolitical shifts, what comes through most strongly is that Sorrell still genuinely enjoys the business.

Asked what advice he’d give someone starting today, he reaches back to something his father told him decades ago: find an industry you love, build a reputation and then start something of your own.

“If you love what you do, if you enjoy what you do, this work-life balance thing… You work seven days a week and it isn’t work.”

That enthusiasm perhaps explains why he still believes agencies have a future, despite all the disruption. They expose people to different industries, different problems and different technologies in a way few other professions can. “You deal with multiple verticals. One day it’s a financial institution, one day it’s a packaged goods company, one day it’s a tech platform.”

Asked whether he’d start another advertising business today, Sorrell gently corrects the question. “When you say advertising,” he smiles, “I modify it to tech-led advertising.”

Five years from now, he believes the Croisette will look very different. “I think we’ll walk down the Croisette and find it totally dominated by technology and that’s very difficult for Don Draper to swallow.”
