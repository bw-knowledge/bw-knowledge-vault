# Startups are clamping down on internal AI slop: ‘You’re losing so much’

## AI writing policies could become commonplace in startups, as people tire of slop

* * *

[Miriam Partington](https://sifted.eu/author/miriam-partington/)

5 min read

Share

![](https://images.sifted.eu/cdn-cgi/image/width=3840,quality=75,fit=cover,format=auto,gravity=auto,height=1280/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/19155002/ChatGPT-Image-Jun-19-2026-03_49_46-PM-e1781880722984.png)

If you’ve spent enough time online lately, you’re likely familiar with the quirks of AI-generated writing: from the overt use of m-dashes and hyperbole to the “it’s not just x, it’s y.”

As startup employees increasingly use AI to write documents, emails and Slack messages,

internal communication problems are fast emerging — with CEOs hurriedly drawing up new policies to combat them.

Last week, San Francisco-based sales intelligence startup Clay published its [AI Writing Policy](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/vaanand_we-just-instituted-an-official-ai-writing-share-7492584308881330176-rcmv/), encouraging employees to think first before using AI to write. In one of its guiding principles, it holds employees responsible for ensuring a document reflects their own thoughts before sharing it.

Advertisement

“If a reviewer asks, “What did you mean by this line?” it’s not acceptable to reply with “Oh sorry, AI wrote that, ignore it,” the policy authored by engineer Sophie Alpert reads.

Dutch startup Polarstep also published its own [AI writing policy](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/clare-jones-_ai-writing-can-be-painful-to-read-it-can-activity-7467101885594914816-Ityf/?utm_medium=ios_app&rcm=ACoAAAgWSGABeB-S24WW75ybdFoMfK9elr-CNmM&utm_source=social_share_send&utm_campaign=copy_link) this summer. CEO Clare Jones noticed that documents were getting longer and less comprehensible, and messages between team members were often jumbled.

“It was harder to understand what each other was trying to say, because \[the writing\] had all those words like “genuinely” and “directionally positive” — and oh my gosh, “quietly” appeared in everything,” she told Sifted. “I was having to squint at the document to really understand what’s being said — you know, what’s the thing behind the drama?”

### **Sloppifying comms**

In a fast-moving startup environment, communicating clearly and concisely is important to keep teams aligned.

Large language models can’t yet produce accurate, well-sourced text on their own. Even with perfect prompting, LLMs can muddle sentences, exaggerate facts, and, often, make things up, making it all the more important for humans to oversee their output.

Online, startup employees have [raged about colleagues](https://x.com/tednotlasso/status/2076780975581794526) who simply ‘copy paste’ output from chatbots without reading through or editing it first.

Victor Riparbelli, cofounder of AI avatars startup Synthesia, sent a Slack message to his employees in June after noticing AI-slop was sneaking into company documents. While he hasn’t banned the use of LLMs in writing, he’s asked employees to use them more intentionally to avoid wasting time.

“When we write too long and verbose documents we place the burden of distilling the content onto the readers, so instead of one person spending ten mins on sharpening a doc, we have ten people spending ten additional minutes reading slop, leading to net-negative productivity,” he wrote.

Beyond comprehension issues, another commonly cited problem of using AI to write is it can erode individual personality and voice and make everything sound the same.

A recent piece by Harvard Business Review highlighted CEOs risk [“flattening” their leadership style](https://hbr.org/2026/08/dont-let-ai-flatten-your-leadership-style) by using AI to communicate, losing employee trust in the process.

Advertisement

Jones thinks the risk is broader, potentially impacting a team’s ability to connect with one another.

“One of our values is dedication to craft and that is reflected in our app and in its design, but also in how we behave,” says Jones. “How you communicate is also your craft as a team member, as a leader and a manager. And when you outsource that to an LLM that doesn’t know you, or your team, or the company, you’re losing so much.”

For example, Jones says her Dutch colleague used the idiom “cucumber time” in a recent conversation, referring to the quiet summer months when everyone is on holiday and the news cycle is slower — something Jones hadn’t heard before.

“It \[brought\] such a lovely moment of connection, and you miss all that when you screen out all the quirks of language that make us different,” she says.

### **A new industry standard?**

There are some benefits of using AI to communicate in ways other than text.

Large language models can present information in varying formats to suit the individual’s needs. Jones recently had to present an annual review to a colleague who isn’t fond of reading; she recorded herself speaking and used an LLM to make it into a podcast.

Voice AI also enables team members to talk through an idea for a product or sales strategy aloud, while the AI takes notes for them.

“That’s the lovely thing. There are loads of ways you can use AI to be more inclusive to help people get context and information in the way that works best for them,” says Jones.

But regulating the use of AI in writing is likely to persist as people tire of slop. The tech industry itself is moving to set new standards.

This month, Anthropic announced it intends to [watermark text](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/11/anthropic-says-it-will-watermark-text-generated-by-its-ai-models/) generated by its AI models in line with the EU AI Act’s Transparency Code, and LinkedIn launched a “Seems like AI slop” button.

Substack has also partnered up with Pangram to detect AI-generated content in posts and articles, a move which sparked controversy among some users of the platform.

Sarah Drinkwater, solo GP at Common Magic, says AI writing policies are necessary for startups as they help team members distinguish where generating content is appropriate, and where it “creates slop, wastes time and leads to unoriginal thinking.”

“For startups creating cultures that allow them to ship and learn at speed, while creating distinctive products and edge, this deeply matters,” she says.

“I think we’ll see many organisations, from companies outside tech to schools and governments, adopt AI writing policies to set their parameters and create trust internally and externally.”
